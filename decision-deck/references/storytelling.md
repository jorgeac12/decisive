# Storytelling with data for executives

Read before building the storyboard. These rules synthesize Barbara Minto (Pyramid Principle and SCQA), the action-title practice of strategy consulting, Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic (Storytelling with Data), Nancy Duarte (DataStory) and Stephen Few.

## 1. The Big Idea (before any slide)

Write one sentence that has:
- **A point of view:** what you argue, not the topic.
- **What is at stake:** what is gained or lost.
- **A complete sentence,** with a verb.

Weak: "New customer service architecture". Strong: "Merging the service channels only cuts cost if three owner decisions come before the pilot".

Three-minute test: if you had three minutes and no slides, could you tell the story? Write that short text. It becomes the executive summary.

## 2. The opening: SCQA

The summary slide follows this order:
1. **Situation:** context the audience already accepts.
2. **Complication:** what changed or what problem appeared.
3. **Question:** what the audience needs to answer.
4. **Answer:** your recommendation, stated directly.

The answer comes at the start, not at the end. Executives want the conclusion first and the arguments after (Minto's pyramid). Known risk: if the audience rejects the recommendation up front, they tend to discard the rest, so it must be backed by evidence in the following slides.

## 3. The body: pyramid and MECE groups

- Each argument supports the answer. Each argument has about three pieces of evidence.
- Groups do not overlap and together cover the problem (MECE: mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive).
- Deck sections are the argument groups, not the chapters of the source document.

## 4. Action titles

The title is the slide's conclusion, not its topic.

| Rule | Weak | Strong |
|---|---|---|
| Complete sentence with a verb | Branch results | Two of our five branches now carry the whole region |
| Specific | Sales changed | Sales grew 18% in five months |
| Quantified | Supplier status | A 10-day supplier delay pushes the store opening into April |
| Outcome, not activity | We analyzed the pipelines | Three pipelines cause 60% of the delay |
| Two lines at most | (a paragraph in the title) | (the caveat goes in the body) |

Test: if the title would be true for any company in any year, it is not finished.

## 5. Horizontal and vertical logic

- **Horizontal:** read only the titles, in order. They must tell the whole story, like a paragraph. If they do not, the storyline is wrong, not the design.
- **Vertical:** everything in the slide body supports its title. What does not support it leaves the slide or goes to the notes.
- Write every title BEFORE building any slide (storyboard). Show the storyboard in the analysis.

## 6. From data to recommendation

For each relevant number, go down three steps:
1. **Observation:** what the data shows ("response time fell 25%").
2. **Implication:** why it matters ("the yearly target is now reachable").
3. **Recommendation:** what to do ("keep the new process and measure for one more quarter").

A chart without an implication is decoration. An implication without a recommendation is a report.

## 7. Context for every number

A number alone means nothing. Always give at least one comparison:
- against **target** or budget;
- against the **previous period** (month, quarter, year);
- against a **benchmark** (external or another team).

And always: **unit, period, source and cut-off date** (the `source` line under the chart). Estimates get "estimate, validate before quoting".

## 8. Directing attention (preattentive attributes)

The brain sees color, size and position before it reads. Use that on purpose:
- **Gray everything, color one thing.** In a chart, what is not the message is gray. The message is the accent color. The components do this with `highlight`.
- **Size:** the main number is the largest element on the slide.
- **Position:** the most important item sits top left, where the eye starts.
- **Annotation:** write on the chart what the audience should see ("new campaign starts") instead of hoping they find it.
- **Short memory:** people hold about four visual chunks at once. Beyond that, split the slide.

## 9. Remove clutter

Anything that does not help the message hurts it:
- No separate legend: label the end of the line or the side of the bar.
- No heavy gridlines, 3D, shadows or chart borders.
- No pie with more than three slices (use `stack` or `bar`).
- No truncated axis on bars or columns: bars always start at zero.
- No decimals nobody will use.

## 10. Rhythm and tension

A good deck alternates between what is and what could be (Duarte): problem, possibility, problem, possibility, ending in the new state with the ask. In practice:
- alternate dense slides (table, chart) with breathing slides (callout, big number, section break);
- place the complication early and the resolution at the end;
- end with action, never with "thank you" or "questions?".
